The problem

A launch message survives five hops. Nobody sees where it breaks.

L'Oréal writes a claim once, in Paris, in one language, cleared by legal. By the time a customer hears it, it has passed through zone, market, distributor, and an advisor standing at a shelf — usually in a different language than it was written in.

Each hop is a re-telling, and every re-telling is an unlogged opportunity for the claim to drift: a percentage rounded up, a hedge dropped, a cosmetic benefit nudged into medical language. Today the drift is invisible. Nobody can point to where a message mutated, and nobody can prove it didn't.

Training doesn't close this. A deck read once in a hotel ballroom is not evidence, and a quiz score is not proof that an advisor can hold the approved wording under pressure from a real customer asking a real objection.

The gap is not knowledge. It is that nothing between HQ and the shop floor is observable.

The product

Four surfaces, one governed loop

Beauty Coach is not a chatbot bolted onto a product catalogue. It is a closed loop: the product team publishes a launch, advisors rehearse it against a digital human, every rehearsal is scored with evidence, and HQ sees the truth roll up by market.

What makes it different

The coach cannot make anything up

This is the design decision everything else follows from. The coach answers only from the published launch pack. Approved claims are quoted verbatim — never paraphrased, never summarised, never translated at runtime.

Language is governance, not translation

An approved claim is a regulatory artifact for one market in one language. Machine-translating it does not produce a translated claim; it produces an unapproved one. So packs are authored per language and carry their own status — source, market-approved, or draft — and a draft is never spoken.

Ask for a language that isn't signed off and the coach refuses to start. It names what is missing and who has to approve it. That refusal is the product working, not failing — it is the only way a system like this belongs anywhere near a regulated claim.

Four divisions, four definitions of "ready"

A pharmacist and a hypermarket promoter are not the same advisor and must not be graded the same way. The rubric is weighted per division: Dermatological Beauty weights product accuracy heavily and upselling lightly, so a pharmacist who sells beautifully but misstates a clinical claim fails. Consumer Products inverts it. Each division also injects its own guardrail into the coach and role-plays its own counter.

Every score carries its evidence

Scores are not opinions. Each dimension cites the advisor's own words back to them, and dimensions the session never exercised are reported as untested rather than filled in. That is what makes a scorecard defensible to the advisor being scored and useful to the manager reading it.

How it helps L'Oréal

What each side of the house gets

The same loop pays off differently depending on where you sit. This is the part worth reading twice.

Product & marketing

Your launch message actually arrives

You write the positioning once and it reaches every advisor in every market unchanged — not a summary of a summary made by someone who saw the deck in March. When you change a claim, the change is live in the next session, not in the next training cycle.

And you find out which of your claims advisors can't carry. If nobody in three markets ever repeats your hero claim, that is a message problem you learn in week two instead of after the launch.

GainLaunch messaging lands intact, and weak claims surface before the launch, not after.

Regulatory & legal

A claim can no longer drift quietly

The coach quotes approved wording verbatim and refuses to speak any language pack a market has not signed off. Forbidden wording is scored against explicitly, so an advisor drifting toward treatment or cure language is caught and corrected in rehearsal — not in front of a customer, and not in a regulator's complaint file.

Every pack is versioned and every session is logged. For the first time there is an audit trail between the claim you approved and the words an advisor actually used.

GainClaim compliance becomes observable and auditable instead of assumed.

Zone & market GM

You see readiness before the launch, not after the numbers

Sell-out tells you a launch went badly a month late. Readiness tells you which markets, territories and distributors are cold while you can still do something — reallocate coaching, delay a market, or push a distributor.

Because scoring is division-weighted, a market's readiness number means the same thing whether it is a pharmacy network or a hypermarket chain.

GainA leading indicator of launch execution, weeks ahead of sell-out data.

Distributor & L&D

Training that scales past the trainer

A market trainer can run a handful of sessions a week. The coach runs them in parallel, at any hour, in the advisor's own language, and never gets tired of the same objection. High-turnover channels stop being the place where launch knowledge goes to die.

Certification stops being attendance. An advisor is certified because they held the approved wording under pressure, with the transcript to show for it.

GainCoaching capacity decoupled from headcount; certification backed by evidence.

The advisor

Somewhere safe to be wrong

A private session where you can fumble the objection three times, in your own language, with nobody watching and no customer lost. The coach corrects gently, hands you the approved sentence, and makes you say it back.

Advisors who feel prepared sell better and stay longer. That is the least measurable benefit here and probably not the smallest.

GainConfidence at the counter, built by rehearsal rather than by reading.

The group

A product-knowledge asset that compounds

Every launch published into the Nexus stays there, versioned, in every approved language. Over two or three years that becomes L'Oréal's structured product-knowledge library — the canonical answer to "what are we allowed to say about this product, in this market, right now."

That asset is worth more than the training it was built for. It is the substrate for advisor apps, consumer-facing assistants, and internal search.

GainA durable, structured claims library — reusable far beyond coaching.

Deployment

How it lands in a L'Oréal environment

The coach runs as a web application — no app install for advisors, which matters in channels where the device is a shared shop tablet or the advisor's own phone. Access is by distributor-issued code, so country, territory and distributor are established at sign-in rather than typed in and mistyped.

Data residency is a first-class setting, not an afterthought. Speech transcription can point at a hosted service for a pilot or at a Whisper-compatible endpoint inside L'Oréal's own network for markets where advisor voice recordings cannot leave the perimeter. The same applies to the pack store.

Launch content enters through the Nexus and belongs to L'Oréal. There is no scenario where the coaching library is locked inside a vendor's format.

Proposed pilot

One launch, two markets, ninety days

The fastest way to prove this is not a broad rollout. It is a single real launch, run properly, in two markets that differ enough to be a real test — ideally one Dermatological Beauty market in SAPMENA and one Consumer Products market in North Asia, so both the strictest claim regime and the highest-volume channel are exercised.

What we would measure

Claim-fidelity rate (share of advisors who can carry the hero claim verbatim), forbidden-wording incidence before and after coaching, time-to-certification per advisor, and coverage — what share of the target advisor base rehearsed at all. The last one is the honest test of whether advisors find it useful or merely mandated.

One thing to settle early: who signs off a language pack. The system enforces that someone does — whether that is zone regulatory, market legal, or the local brand GM differs across L'Oréal's zones, and it determines who holds the approver role in the Nexus. Worth deciding in week one rather than discovering in week six.
