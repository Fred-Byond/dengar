Beauty Coach — Ecosystem Deep Dive

L'Oréal is not one training problem.
It is four distribution businesses that share a logo.

Where the brand portfolio, the channel, and the claim-approval regime actually sit — and how the coach was rebuilt to serve all four divisions in six languages without ever inventing a claim.

Prepared for BYOND Asia 4 divisions · ~37 brands · 6 languages Status: built & verified

The premise

The message-consistency problem is really a claims-governance problem

The brief was: make sure new-launch messaging reaches the consumer intact, in any language. Once you look at how L'Oréal actually reaches the consumer, that sentence stops being a training problem and becomes a governance one.

A L'Oréal product claim is not marketing copy. It is a regulatory artifact, reviewed and approved for one specific market, in one specific language, under one specific regime. “Tested under dermatological control on sensitive skin” passes in an EU pharmacy. The same sentence, machine-translated into Arabic for a Gulf pharmacy, is not the approved claim — it is a new, unapproved claim that nobody signed off.

So the risk isn't that advisors forget the message. It's that every hop — HQ to zone, zone to distributor, distributor to promoter, promoter to customer, in a different language each time — is an unlogged opportunity for the claim to mutate. Today nobody can see where it mutated, or prove it didn't.

The product is not a chatbot that knows about beauty. It is the only place in the chain where the approved claim is spoken back, in language, and the deviation is recorded.

Structure — the four divisions

Different advisor, different counter, different regulator

L'Oréal reports through four operating divisions. They are not market segments of one business — they are four separate routes to the consumer, with different people standing at the end of each one. A single coaching persona and a single scorecard cannot serve all four.

CPD — Consumer Products

Mass & drugstore

The advisor is a retail promoter or brand ambassador, often employed by the distributor, working a shelf in a hypermarket or drugstore.

Channel
Hypermarkets, supermarkets, drugstores, e-commerce marketplaces
Contact
Seconds. High footfall, low dwell, frequently no consultation at all
Claim regime
General cosmetic advertising rules; heavy scrutiny of performance percentages and before/after language
Fails first
Over-claiming to close fast; inventing a benefit the pack doesn't carry

Luxe — L'Oréal Luxe

Selective & travel retail

The advisor is a counter beauty advisor in a department store, perfumery, boutique or airport — trained, uniformed, on a service ritual.

Channel
Department stores, perfumeries, DTC boutiques, travel retail, brand.com
Contact
Long. Consultation, diagnosis, ritual, sampling, gifting
Claim regime
Cosmetic rules plus tight brand-codes: tone, vocabulary and story are as governed as the claim
Fails first
Off-brand register — the right fact in the wrong voice devalues the product

Derm — Dermatological Beauty

Pharmacy & medical

The advisor is a pharmacist, pharmacy assistant or clinic staffer, and is often recommending to a customer with an actual skin condition.

Channel
Pharmacies, parapharmacies, dermatology and aesthetic clinics, medical e-comm
Contact
Advisory, repeat, condition-led. The customer may already be on a prescription
Claim regime
Strictest. Health-adjacent wording, clinical-study substantiation, and a hard cosmetic/medicine boundary that varies by market
Fails first
Drifting into treatment or cure language — the one failure with regulatory consequences

PPD — Professional Products

Salon & stylist

The advisor is a hairdresser or colourist — a professional who both uses the product and sells it, and who owns the client relationship outright.

Channel
Salons, salon distributors, professional academies and education networks
Contact
Captive — 45 minutes in the chair, high trust, strong repeat
Claim regime
Professional/technical substantiation: process, mixing ratios, service protocol as much as end-benefit
Fails first
Technical error in the service, then reciting consumer marketing to a professional peer

Geographically these run through five reporting zones — Europe, North America, North Asia, SAPMENA-SSA (South Asia Pacific, Middle East, North Africa, Sub-Saharan Africa) and Latin America. A single brand can therefore sit under four different claim regimes in four zones at once.

Portfolio — the brand tree

~37 international brands, each inheriting its division's rules

This is the part that breaks a naive build. “L'Oréal” in the product picker is meaningless — an advisor never sells L'Oréal, they sell Kérastase to a salon client or CeraVe to a pharmacy customer. The brand is what determines the division, and the division is what determines how the coach must behave. So brand is modelled as a first-class entity that resolves to a division, not as a text label on a product.

The Shu Uemura case is the reason brand alone isn't enough. Shu Uemura makeup is Luxe, sold at a department-store counter. Shu Uemura Art of Hair is PPD, sold by a stylist in a salon. Same brand name, two divisions, two advisor types, two vocabularies. The coach resolves division from the product, not the brand label — which is why divisionId sits on the product record and the brand only supplies its default.

Integration — what varies, what must not

Three axes flex by division. The claim never does.

The temptation is to build four products. The correct move is one engine with three declared variation points, so a new division or brand is configuration rather than a fork.

AxisWhat it changesWorked example
Rubric weights How the six readiness dimensions are weighted when the session is scored, so “ready” means something different per division. Derm weights Product Accuracy ×1.6 and Upsell Fluency ×0.6 — a pharmacist who upsells beautifully but misstates a clinical claim must fail. CPD inverts it.
Persona guardrail A division-specific rule injected into the coach's system prompt alongside the standing guardrails. Derm: never cross into treatment or cure language. Luxe: hold brand register — the fact is not enough. PPD: lead with service protocol before end-benefit.
Channel vocabulary Who the advisor is and where they're standing, stated to the coach so the role-play is the right role-play. The coach role-plays a pharmacy counter for La Roche-Posay and a salon chair for Kérastase — not a generic “customer”.
The approved claim Invariant Quoted verbatim from the launch pack. Never paraphrased, never translated at runtime, never generated. This is the whole point of the product.

Language — governance, not translation

Six languages, chosen because they are five zones

The six languages aren't a demo flourish — between them they cover every L'Oréal reporting zone and the three hardest scripts a digital human has to handle: right-to-left, ideographic, and Devanagari.

The rule that makes this different from “add i18n”

A launch pack is stored per language, not translated on the fly, and each carries a governance status. The coach will only open a session on a pack the market has signed off.

StatusMeaningCoach behaviour
sourceThe original authored pack. English today.Speaks it.
approvedLocalised and signed off by that market's regulatory/legal reviewer in the Nexus.Speaks it.
draftTranslated but not yet market-approved.Refuses the session and names who has to approve it.

A refusal is a feature. Silence is the only safe answer when the approved wording doesn't exist yet.

This is enforced at one chokepoint — getRunnablePack() — rather than sprinkled through the UI, so there is no code path that opens a coaching session on unapproved wording. And it fixed a subtler bug found in testing: the coach's own framing (“That's a common one — here's the approved way to answer it”) was still English inside an Arabic session, which both breaks the illusion and teaches the advisor a bilingual habit they can't use on the shop floor. Framing is now localised alongside the claim.

Honest gaps

Two things to resolve before a client demo

Voice coverage is unproven for five of six languages. The digital human's speech layer has a confirmed voice for English only; Arabic, French, Chinese, Japanese and Hindi are recorded as null in the language table rather than guessed. Text and captions are fully localised today — the avatar will speak reliably only in English until each voice is confirmed with the vendor. That check should happen before any live L'Oréal demo, because a coach that reads Arabic in an English voice is worse than no Arabic at all.

Who signs the approval is a client decision. The system enforces that someone approves a translation before it can be spoken. Whether that person is zone regulatory, market legal, or the local brand GM differs across L'Oréal's zones, and it changes who gets the Nexus approver role. Worth putting on the agenda rather than assuming.

Natural next steps

Extend the catalogue past four pilot products into a real division-balanced set; add zone as a first-class dimension above market so HQ can compare SAPMENA against North Asia; and upgrade session scoring from keyword rubric to an LLM judge that cites the verbatim approved claim as evidence for every score it gives.
